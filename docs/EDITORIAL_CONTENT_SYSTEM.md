# Joshua's Point — Editorial Content System

## Status and purpose

This handbook is the editorial source of truth for anyone researching, writing, photographing,
reviewing, or publishing Joshua's Point content. It applies to destinations, dive sites, scooter
routes, journal stories, practical guides, captions, metadata, and future editorial products.

It complements:

- [`design-system/08-writing-style.md`](./design-system/08-writing-style.md) for the brand voice.
- [`SANITY_CONTENT_MODEL.md`](./SANITY_CONTENT_MODEL.md) for content fields and publishing states.
- [`DESTINATION_SYSTEM.md`](./DESTINATION_SYSTEM.md) for destination architecture and governance.
- [`RELATIONSHIP_ENGINE.md`](./RELATIONSHIP_ENGINE.md) for curated and automatic relationships.

This document defines editorial standards, not CMS schemas or frontend layouts. A field being
available in the CMS does not make its content publishable. Every published piece must meet the
standards and publish gate in this handbook.

## How to use this handbook

1. Choose the relevant content standard before research begins.
2. Create a fact sheet and source record separately from the narrative draft.
3. Write the story before compressing it into excerpts, metadata, or social copy.
4. Complete specialist, factual, photographic, accessibility, and editorial review.
5. Score the piece using Section 11.
6. Publish only when every critical gate and checklist item passes.
7. Re-review volatile facts on schedule while preserving the enduring story.

## 1. Editorial Mission

Joshua's Point exists to become the most trusted guide to Southern Negros.

Every article should help people travel slowly, responsibly, and confidently. Trust is earned
through original observation, local knowledge, factual restraint, useful detail, beautiful
photography, visible review dates, and prompt corrections. It is not earned by publishing the most
pages or making the strongest claims.

### The editorial promise

> We describe the place honestly, explain the journey clearly, and leave the reader free to choose.

### What every piece should do

A reader should finish with:

- A clear sense of the place, not merely a list of attributes.
- Enough practical context to decide whether it suits them.
- An understanding of what remains uncertain or must be checked locally.
- Respect for the landscape, community, and people connected to it.
- A natural path to nearby or related reading when genuinely useful.

### Editorial values

1. **Accuracy before speed.** It is better to publish later than to publish a guess.
2. **Observation before promotion.** Describe what is there and how it feels.
3. **Local knowledge before aggregation.** First-hand reporting and qualified local sources carry
   more weight than copied summaries.
4. **Context before convenience.** Travel time, weather, access, etiquette, and limitations belong
   beside inspiration.
5. **Care before reach.** Sensitive places are not exposed for traffic.
6. **Specificity before superlatives.** Concrete detail is more trustworthy than praise.
7. **Transparency before certainty.** Say when something is unknown, variable, or not recently
   verified.

### Trust boundaries

Joshua's Point is an editorial guide, not a navigation service, tour operator, dive operator,
emergency resource, or guarantee of access and conditions. Content should help readers ask better
questions and make better decisions without pretending to replace official notices, qualified
professionals, local advice, or their own judgment.

## 2. Editorial Tone

Joshua's Point writes like a thoughtful host who knows the place well: calm, observant, warm,
curious, confident, and humble. The writing invites attention. It does not demand it.

### Writing principles

- Use plain, natural English.
- Prefer short and medium sentences, with occasional longer sentences for rhythm.
- Build paragraphs around one clear observation.
- Use active voice when it identifies responsibility or makes instructions clearer.
- Name real materials, weather, sounds, terrain, people, and moments.
- Separate observed experience from verified fact and personal recommendation.
- Use “may,” “often,” “typically,” or a date when conditions vary—but never use qualification to
  disguise poor research.
- Explain local terms on first use without flattening their meaning.
- Let quiet details carry emotion.
- End before the writing begins to persuade.

### Editorial point of view

- Use **third person** for factual and descriptive guidance.
- Use **second person** sparingly for clear practical orientation: “Bring water for the return.”
- Use **first person plural** only when Joshua's Point genuinely owns the observation or
  recommendation: “We prefer to leave before the road becomes busy.”
- Name the source when a statement belongs to a guide, operator, resident, authority, or business.
- Do not use an invented collective voice to make an unsupported claim sound established.

### Headlines

Headlines should be short, specific, and calm. They may create curiosity, but they must not conceal
the subject.

**Good**

- Casaroro Falls
- The Road into the Mountains
- First Light on the Eastern Coast
- A Morning at the Market
- Where the Forest Meets the Water

**Avoid**

- You Won't Believe This Secret Waterfall
- 10 Unmissable Things to Do in Negros
- The Ultimate Southern Negros Bucket List
- The Most Beautiful Dive Site in the Philippines
- Everything You Need to Know

### Introductions

An introduction should establish place, atmosphere, and relevance in two or three paragraphs. It
should not summarize every section or begin with a generic claim about beauty.

**Good pattern**

> The first impression is not the destination itself, but the change in the journey: the air
> cooling, the road narrowing, and the sound of water arriving before the view. **[Use only with
> verified first-hand observation.]**

**Practical alternative when observation is limited**

> Casaroro Falls sits within the mountain landscape west of Dumaguete. This guide brings together
> the character of the visit and the practical details that should be checked before setting out.

**Avoid**

> Nestled in paradise, this breathtaking hidden gem is a must-visit destination that will leave you
> speechless.

### Storytelling

Build a story from a sequence of meaningful observations:

1. Departure or orientation.
2. Change in landscape, light, weather, or pace.
3. Arrival and first impression.
4. The defining detail or encounter.
5. Reflection grounded in the place.

Do not force this into every article. The sequence is a lens, not a formula. A story should contain
scenes and details that the writer or an attributed source actually experienced.

**Prefer**

> Rain changes the character of the road. The views close in, the forest feels nearer, and the
> final approach asks for more time.

**Avoid**

> Whether you're an adventure seeker or a nature lover, there is something here for everyone.

### Practical information

Practical copy should be compact, qualified, dated, and easy to scan. It must distinguish the
source, the verified date, and natural variability.

**Good**

> **Travel time:** Allow approximately **[verified range]** from Joshua's Point in ordinary daytime
> conditions. Recheck the route after heavy rain. Last verified **[date]**.

> **Opening pattern:** **[Verified hours or pattern]** according to **[named source]**, checked
> **[date]**. Contact the site directly before a special journey.

**Avoid**

> It is only a quick ride away and is open daily.

The word “only” minimizes the journey; “quick” is subjective; “daily” becomes misinformation when
it has no source or review date.

### Closing paragraphs

A closing should return to the article's central observation. It may offer a quiet recommendation,
but should not turn into a sales pitch or generic call to action.

**Good**

> The place is best remembered not as a stop completed, but as a change in pace—the road in, the
> cooler air, and the time given to the return.

**Avoid**

> What are you waiting for? Add it to your bucket list and book your stay today.

### Words and claims

Prefer specific language over words such as “beautiful,” “amazing,” or “unique.” If a superlative
cannot be measured, sourced, and needed, remove it. The established brand avoids “luxury,”
“exclusive,” “premium,” “world-class,” “perfect,” and “paradise” except where factual context truly
requires the term.

### What we never do

- **Clickbait:** no withheld subjects, manufactured surprise, fear, outrage, or curiosity gaps.
- **Exaggerated marketing:** no “must-see,” “once-in-a-lifetime,” “untouched,” “secret,” or
  unsupported “best.”
- **AI-sounding copy:** no polished but empty introductions, symmetrical filler paragraphs,
  repetitive conclusions, generic sensory language, or phrases that could describe any place.
- **Keyword stuffing:** no unnatural repetition of “Southern Negros,” destination names, nearby
  towns, or activity terms.
- **Unnecessary superlatives:** no rankings or absolutes without a rigorous editorial reason and
  source.
- Fabricated quotations, observations, anecdotes, local terms, or personal experience.
- Copied facts without tracing them to a current, accountable source.
- False precision, including exact travel times or visibility presented without context.
- Stereotypes, exoticizing language, poverty tourism, or treating communities as scenery.
- Directions to sensitive ecological, cultural, private, or unsafe locations for the sake of reach.
- Undisclosed paid inclusion, complimentary treatment, affiliate relationships, or conflicts.

## 3. Destination Writing Standard

A destination page is a field-informed editorial guide, not an attraction listing. It should
combine an enduring story with a clearly separated layer of volatile practical information.

### Required editorial structure

#### 1. Identity and orientation

- Correct public name and spelling.
- Destination type and region.
- Concise excerpt that works without promotional adjectives.
- Map location at the appropriate precision.
- Hero photograph that represents the actual experience.

#### 2. Why visit

Answer who the place may suit and what kind of time it offers. Give the reader a reason grounded in
experience: landscape, journey, quiet, culture, ecology, craft, food, or light.

Do not write “everyone should visit.” A useful guide also helps readers decide not to go.

#### 3. What makes it special

Identify one or two defining qualities and support them with observation or context. “Special” is
not a licence for superlatives.

**Weak:** “It is one of the most stunning places in Negros.”

**Stronger:** “The experience is shaped by the transition from open road to enclosed forest, and by
how little of the water is visible until the final approach.” **[Publish only if observed and
verified.]**

#### 4. Arrival experience

Describe the final approach and what a visitor encounters:

- Where the journey meaningfully changes.
- The likely first view, sound, terrain, or point of orientation.
- What is not obvious from a map.
- Whether the final access requires walking, permission, a guide, a boat, steps, or uncertain
  terrain.

Arrival writing is both emotional and practical. Never romanticize a hazard.

#### 5. Practical information

Include only relevant, verified facts:

- Approximate travel-time range from Joshua's Point.
- Recommended transport and realistic alternatives.
- Location label and directions handoff.
- Access and mobility considerations.
- Current fee and what it covers, or “not verified.”
- Opening hours or visiting pattern, or “not verified.”
- Facilities, parking, fuel, signal, guides, permits, or reservations where relevant.
- Things to bring.
- Factual source and last-verified date.

Unknown fields remain empty or are labeled unknown. Do not infer a fee, opening time, facility, or
access rule from silence.

#### 6. Best time to visit

Explain time of day and season only when supported by observation or reliable local knowledge.
Separate:

- Light and photography preference.
- Heat, rain, tide, river, or trail conditions.
- Crowding or community rhythms.
- Formal access schedules.

Avoid promising weather or conditions. “Best” must be contextual: best for what, according to whom,
and verified when?

#### 7. Photography notes

Offer useful, respectful guidance:

- Direction and character of light.
- Likely spray, rain, low light, or equipment protection.
- Whether a wider or vertical composition tells the place more honestly.
- Permission, privacy, drone, flash, tripod, and habitat considerations.
- Moments when the camera should remain away.

Do not turn the location into a shot list or encourage unsafe positioning.

#### 8. Tips

Tips reduce uncertainty and support respectful travel. Keep each tip concise and sourced where
factual. Prioritize timing, preparation, etiquette, alternatives, and reasons to turn back.

Do not repeat universal advice merely to make the page longer.

#### 9. Nearby places

Recommend a small number of places that genuinely fit the same journey, geography, time of day, or
editorial story. “Nearby” must not mean coordinates alone. A river, road condition, ferry, opening
pattern, or mountain route may make a geometrically close place impractical.

Use existing relationships rather than repeating the full description. Explain the connection in
one sentence when it is not obvious.

#### 10. Joshua's Point recommendation

This is a host's considered note, not a promotional badge. It should answer one or more of:

- Who will appreciate the journey.
- When Joshua's Point would leave and how much of the day to allow.
- What to pair with it without overfilling the day.
- When another destination or transport choice would be wiser.
- The single detail worth giving time to.

Label it consistently as **From Joshua's Point** or another approved editorial title. Never use
“Joshua's Point recommends” to imply safety certification, guaranteed access, or a commercial
endorsement.

### Recommended length

Length follows reporting depth, not a word target. As a working range, a complete destination guide
may use 800–1,500 words of narrative and 150–350 words of practical guidance. Publish a shorter,
complete piece rather than padding it to reach the range.

### Destination publish gate

Do not publish until the place has been visited by the writer or a named contributor, unless the
piece is explicitly framed as desk-researched and the editor approves that limitation. GPS,
access, fee, hours, transport, sensitive-location precision, and review date must pass Section 8.

## 4. Dive Site Writing Standard

A dive-site page is an editorial field guide supported by qualified technical review. It should
convey the character of the dive without promising sightings or treating variable conditions as a
specification sheet.

### Required editorial structure

1. **Identity and regional orientation** — Verified public site name, region, entry type, and
   appropriately precise location.
2. **Editorial introduction** — What gives the site its character and who may appreciate it.
3. **Entry and descent** — Shore, boat, or mixed entry; the transition into the dive; relevant
   orientation without replacing a briefing.
4. **Underwater story** — Topography, habitat, light, movement, and the sequence of the experience.
5. **Marine life** — Dated observations and habitat context without guarantees.
6. **Conditions** — Depth, visibility, current, temperature when supported, level, season, and
   variability.
7. **Photography** — Subject scale, light, visibility, current, etiquette, and equipment exposure.
8. **Safety** — Required qualified review, limits, local briefing, and reasons conditions may change.
9. **Nearby places** — Relevant dive sites and on-land destinations that form a coherent journey.
10. **Closing observation** — A restrained conclusion grounded in the dive's character.

### Marine life standard

- Use accepted common names; add scientific names only when identification is reliable and useful.
- Attribute unusual or historical sightings.
- State observation date or season when it matters.
- Use “observed,” “recorded,” “may be seen,” or habitat language; never promise a sighting.
- Do not disclose precise locations of vulnerable species, nesting areas, or sensitive habitat.
- Avoid “abundant,” “rare,” and “common” unless defined by a qualified source or repeated records.
- Do not encourage touching, feeding, chasing, crowding, collecting, or manipulating marine life for
  photographs.

### Conditions standard

Conditions are contextual observations, not guarantees.

- Depths use metric units and distinguish maximum from average or typical profile.
- Visibility is a range plus context and verified date, not a single permanent number.
- Current uses the approved vocabulary and explains variability.
- Dive level follows the approved controlled terms, but the text must say that an operator decides
  suitability for the conditions and diver.
- “Best season” describes a pattern supported by local expertise, not certainty.
- Never extrapolate current conditions from an old log, a single dive, or another nearby site.

### Photography standard for dive content

- Explain wide-angle, macro, ambient-light, or low-light character only from real observation.
- Include visibility and current implications without providing unsafe technique advice.
- Name restrictions on flash, lights, approach, or protected subjects where applicable.
- Captions identify what is shown and when observed; alt text communicates the image without
  promising the same sighting.
- Color correction should restore a natural underwater impression, not create species, clarity, or
  conditions that were not present.

### Safety standard

- Safety notes must be reviewed by a named, appropriately qualified local dive professional.
- Record reviewer, credentials or role, date, and review scope internally.
- State that the page does not replace operator assessment, briefing, buddy procedures, personal
  certification limits, medical fitness, or current conditions.
- Do not publish operational directions that could encourage unguided access where local practice,
  ownership, conservation, or safety requires an operator or guide.
- Treat missing technical facts as a publication blocker, not an invitation to estimate.

### Nearby content

Related dive sites should share a region, habitat, experience, or realistic outing. Nearby land
destinations can help readers understand the coast but should not be bundled into an overfilled day.
Use curated relationships first and incoming relationships as supporting discovery.

### Dive-site publish gate

The page requires editorial review plus independent qualified review of coordinates, depths,
visibility context, current, entry, level, season, marine-life wording, and safety notes. A general
copy editor cannot approve technical dive facts alone.

## 5. Scooter Route Standard

Scooter guidance should answer: **Is this journey appropriate for me today?** It must not present
Joshua's Point as a rental operator, rider trainer, navigation authority, or guarantor of road
conditions.

### Route description structure

1. **Route purpose** — Where it leads and why the journey is meaningful.
2. **Rider assumption** — Experience, licence, insurance, vehicle suitability, and comfort assumed.
3. **Route character** — Surface, width, traffic, elevation, exposure, junction complexity, weather,
   and final access.
4. **Travel time** — A verified range from Joshua's Point plus the assumptions behind it.
5. **Road quality** — Controlled assessment with a plain-language note.
6. **Fuel and services** — Last reliably verified opportunity, without guaranteeing availability.
7. **Stops** — A small number of safe, meaningful places to pause.
8. **Parking and arrival** — Formality, security, access, and final walking conditions.
9. **Safety and alternatives** — Known concerns, weather effects, daylight, reasons to turn back,
   and another transport option where possible.
10. **Photography** — Safe stopping, light, privacy, and places where photography is inappropriate.
11. **Verification** — Route observer, date, conditions, and vehicle context.

### Travel-time standard

- Use a range, not an exact promise.
- State whether the estimate excludes stops and assumes ordinary daytime conditions.
- Do not derive editorial time from a map provider alone.
- Separate moving time from a realistic half-day or full-day allowance.
- Recheck after construction, severe weather, ferry changes, or credible local reports.

### Road-quality vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Paved | Predominantly sealed surface; does not imply smooth, wide, quiet, or safe |
| Mixed surface | Meaningful combination of sealed and unsealed sections |
| Rough sections | Potholes, broken surface, gravel, mud, crossings, or similar sections materially affect the journey |
| Condition variable | Weather, works, tides, rivers, or maintenance can change suitability quickly |
| Not recently verified | Guidance must not be presented as current |

Every term needs a short route-specific explanation. Avoid a single “good” or “bad” label.

### Difficulty vocabulary

- **Easy:** Predominantly straightforward surfaced roads with ordinary traffic awareness required.
- **Moderate:** Steep, narrow, busy, rough, or navigation-sensitive sections require confident
  riding.
- **Demanding:** Sustained difficult conditions or access constraints; an alternative should be
  presented clearly.

Difficulty assesses the observed route under stated conditions, not the reader. Rain, darkness,
traffic, fatigue, vehicle condition, and road changes can increase it.

### Fuel and stops

- Verify fuel information locally and date it.
- Phrase fuel as preparation, never guaranteed stock or opening.
- Select stops because they provide rest, context, food, fuel, shelter, or a safe view—not to fill a
  route.
- Confirm that suggested stopping and parking are lawful, safe, and respectful of homes and
  businesses.
- Never encourage stopping on blind bends, narrow shoulders, bridges, private land, or unstable
  roadside edges.

### Safety and photography

- Safety-critical changes trigger immediate review or temporary removal of scooter guidance.
- Do not publish turn-by-turn instructions that can become stale unnoticed.
- Do not call a route a challenge, achievement, or adventure badge.
- Photography notes must put safe stopping, traffic awareness, permission, and road position before
  the image.
- Future GPX files require separate versioning, route ownership, correction, and support policies.

### Scooter-route publish gate

The complete route must be locally checked in both directions where the return differs materially.
Record date, time, weather, vehicle type, rider, construction, fuel check, and any unverified
segment. A partial route cannot be presented as fully verified.

## 6. Journal Standard

The journal is for original stories with a clear point of view. It is not a production line for
search traffic or a place to restate destination pages in a different order.

### Story-first standard

Every article begins with a story proposition in one sentence:

> This is a story about **[subject]**, observed through **[specific lens]**, because it helps the
> reader understand **[larger meaning]**.

If the proposition could apply to any destination, it is not ready.

### Recommended structure

1. A precise headline and standfirst.
2. An opening scene, question, person, object, or observation.
3. Context sufficient to understand why the subject matters.
4. A narrative movement rather than a collection of interchangeable sections.
5. Original reporting, first-hand observation, or attributed expertise.
6. Practical information only where it serves the story; link to maintained guides for volatile
   details.
7. A closing that changes or deepens the opening observation.
8. A few genuinely related destinations, dive sites, experiences, rooms, or stories.

### We do not publish

- Listicles assembled around a number.
- Generic “things to do,” “hidden gems,” or “ultimate guide” posts.
- Rewritten press releases, tourism-board copy, or summaries of other blogs.
- Thin seasonal posts created only to target a keyword.
- Destination articles split into multiple pages to increase traffic.
- Interviews without consent, context, fact checking, and an agreed representation of sensitive
  material.
- Personal essays that reveal private family or guest stories without explicit approval.

### Article forms that fit

- A reported place essay.
- A portrait of a maker, guide, cook, diver, or community member with consent.
- A photographic essay with substantial captions and context.
- A seasonal field note grounded in repeated observation.
- An architecture, material, ecology, food, or journey story.
- A responsible-travel explainer written with qualified sources.

### Journal editing questions

- What can this writer tell us that a search result cannot?
- Which scene or detail could only come from being there?
- Whose knowledge made the story possible, and are they represented fairly?
- What has been omitted for privacy, safety, or ecological care?
- Does every section advance the story?
- Would the piece remain worth reading if it brought no search traffic?

## 7. Photography Standard

Photography is evidence, atmosphere, and story. It must show the real relationship between nature,
architecture, people, and light without making a place appear larger, emptier, wilder, clearer, or
more accessible than it was.

### Assignment coverage

A complete field assignment should aim to include:

- One defining landscape image.
- One arrival or orientation image.
- One image that explains scale or terrain.
- One close observation or material detail.
- People or local practice only with appropriate consent.
- Practical access imagery when it helps a reader understand the journey.
- Vertical and horizontal alternatives where the story genuinely supports both.

This is a coverage guide, not a requirement to publish every image.

### Landscape orientation

- Use landscape frames for hero, panoramic context, route, architecture, and relationships between
  elements.
- Compose with enough safe space for responsive crops, but never sacrifice the photograph to a
  hypothetical overlay.
- Keep horizons intentional and distortion restrained.
- Include scale when scale matters to planning or understanding.
- Do not erase ordinary context to manufacture wilderness.

### Vertical usage

- Use vertical photographs for height, descent, trees, waterfalls, people, doorways, details, and
  editorial pacing.
- Do not crop a weak horizontal image into a vertical simply to fill a slot.
- Keep the subject away from destructive crop zones when the image may be used responsively.
- Capture a true vertical alternative in the field when possible.

### Drone standard

- Fly only with required permissions, legal compliance, competent operation, safe weather, and
  respect for local restrictions.
- Do not expose sensitive sites, private homes, sacred places, nesting areas, wildlife, or access
  routes that should remain imprecise.
- Avoid flying over people or disturbing communities and animals.
- Use altitude to explain landscape relationships, not to manufacture spectacle.
- Record flight date, operator, permission, and location internally.
- Never use drone photography to imply ordinary public access where none exists.

### Captions

A caption adds context the image alone cannot provide. It may identify place, direction, time,
season, person, activity, or why the moment matters.

**Good:** “The final approach after morning rain, photographed in June 2026.”

**Weak:** “A beautiful view.”

- Keep captions factual and concise.
- Name people and communities only with consent and correct spelling.
- Credit the photographer or source according to the rights agreement.
- Do not repeat the alt text verbatim.

### Alt text

Alt text communicates the image's purpose in its page context.

- Describe the essential subject, setting, and relationship relevant to the adjacent copy.
- Keep it concise; include color, weather, or spatial detail when meaningful.
- Do not begin with “image of” or add SEO keywords.
- Do not include photographer credit in alt text.
- Use empty alt text only when an image is truly decorative and conveys no additional information.
- Maps, diagrams, and information-rich images need a text equivalent beyond one short alt string.

**Example:** “Narrow forest path descending between wet stone walls.”

### Image quality and editing

- Retain an original archival file and a non-destructive edit master.
- Deliver sufficient resolution for the approved placement; never upscale a visibly inadequate
  source to pass a numeric threshold.
- Focus, motion, noise, and dynamic range should support the editorial intention.
- Edit warm, natural, and timeless. Avoid heavy filters, harsh HDR, oversaturation, artificial sky
  replacement, or aggressive object removal.
- Underwater correction may restore a credible impression but must not fabricate clarity, color,
  species, density, or conditions.
- AI-generated or generatively altered imagery must never be presented as documentary photography.
- Record material compositing, removal, or reconstruction and obtain editorial approval.
- Strip unnecessary sensitive location metadata from public files while preserving the internal
  rights and archive record.

### Rights and consent

Before publication, record photographer, copyright owner, licence scope, credit, consent where
people are identifiable, restrictions, expiry, and paid-product rights if relevant. “Found online”
is never a valid source.

## 8. Fact Verification

### Evidence standard

Every volatile or consequential fact needs:

- The exact claim.
- Source name and contact or URL where appropriate.
- Source type: first-hand observation, official source, qualified professional, business owner,
  local resident, map provider, or secondary publication.
- Verification date.
- Verifier.
- Confidence or unresolved conflict.
- Next review date.

The published page does not need to show the full research log, but it must show a meaningful
last-reviewed date and qualify uncertainty.

### Source hierarchy

Prefer, in order appropriate to the fact:

1. Direct field observation by the assigned writer, photographer, or reviewer.
2. Responsible authority, site manager, landowner, protected-area office, or business owner.
3. Qualified local professional for specialist topics such as dive safety and marine conditions.
4. Two independent, current local sources.
5. Provider data or a reputable secondary source used as corroboration, not sole truth for changing
   local access.

Search summaries, AI output, unsourced social posts, copied directories, and old blog posts are
leads only. They are not verification.

### Facts that must always be verified

| Information | Minimum verification | Publish rule | Normal re-review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GPS coordinates | On-site or authoritative coordinate plus independent map check; confirm public precision | Block publishing if the point or precision is uncertain; reduce precision for sensitive places | Annually, after access changes, or on credible correction |
| Fees | Site operator, authority, or direct current observation; record inclusions and currency | Omit or label unverified rather than estimate | Every 90 days and before seasonal promotion |
| Opening hours | Operator, authority, or direct contact; distinguish formal hours from customary pattern | Omit or qualify if not confirmed within 30 days of first publication | Every 90 days; immediately on notice of change |
| Road access | Complete local route check plus current local corroboration where conditions vary | Show date, vehicle, weather context, and unverified segments; remove unsafe guidance | Every 90 days, after severe weather/works, and before route promotion |
| Dive conditions | Recent local professional review plus dated observation context | Never present as live or guaranteed; technical gaps block publication | At least seasonally and every 90 days for promoted guides |

### Additional verified facts

The same discipline applies to travel time, ferry schedules, permits, parking, fuel, public access,
trail difficulty, accessibility, depth, current, visibility, entry type, marine-life observations,
restaurant hours, allergen claims, conservation restrictions, and contact information.

### Review frequency model

| Tier | Content | Review expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical and event-driven | Safety, closure, prohibited access, severe road damage, dive warnings | Review immediately on a credible report; qualify or unpublish while unresolved |
| Volatile | Fees, hours, fuel, transport, access, route condition, operator details | Normally every 90 days and before prominent reuse |
| Seasonal specialist | Dive patterns, marine observations, tides, waterfall/river context, weather-sensitive routes | At least once per relevant season and with qualified review |
| Stable orientation | GPS, public name, general geography, enduring practical context | Annually or when a correction/access change is reported |
| Enduring narrative | First-hand story, history with stable sources, architecture, reflective writing | Review when materially edited or when underlying context changes |

These are maximum normal intervals, not promises of validity. Higher-risk facts may need shorter
cycles. “Last reviewed” records a real material check, not the date someone opened the document.

### Stale and disputed information

- Never refresh a date without rechecking the underlying claims.
- If a volatile fact passes its review date, remove it, mark it as not recently verified, or queue
  the page for review according to risk.
- If sources conflict, publish the uncertainty only when useful and safe; otherwise omit the claim.
- A credible safety or access report can justify temporary unpublishing before full confirmation.
- Record corrections and notify downstream content owners when the same fact appears elsewhere.
- Preserve the original publication date; use an updated or reviewed date for material changes.

## 9. SEO Philosophy

Write for people first. SEO is the work of making useful original content understandable and
discoverable, not changing it into search-engine copy.

### Search principles

- One page owns one clear subject and canonical URL.
- Use the public place name naturally in the title, introduction, and metadata.
- Answer real planning questions in the body where they belong.
- Prefer original reporting, current practical information, strong photography, and local context
  over content volume.
- Keep titles descriptive rather than sensational.
- Write metadata as a truthful summary, not an advertisement.
- Do not add paragraphs solely to repeat a target phrase or nearby town names.
- Avoid duplicate destination, category, journal, and guide pages competing for the same intent.

### Natural internal linking

- Link when another published page genuinely answers the reader's next question.
- Use descriptive anchor text such as “the route toward Lake Balanan,” not “click here.”
- Prefer curated relationships where editorial intent matters.
- Use incoming relationships for discovery without duplicating manual references.
- Do not force a link to every room, destination, experience, or article.
- Check that linked practical content is current enough to recommend.

### Structured data

Structured data must describe visible, accurate content. Depending on the page, future
implementation may use `Article`, `BlogPosting`, `BreadcrumbList`, `ImageObject`, `Place`, or other
appropriate types. Never add ratings, prices, events, FAQs, authors, or geographic detail that the
reader cannot verify on the page.

Editorial staff owns the truth of structured facts; implementation owns valid technical output.
Schema markup is not permission to expose sensitive coordinates.

### Evergreen content

- Separate enduring narrative from volatile fact modules.
- Keep stable URLs and redirect intentionally when slugs must change.
- Show original publication and meaningful review dates.
- Update a page when the reader benefits, not merely to signal freshness.
- Preserve material correction history.
- Do not change dates or repackage old work as new without substantial editorial change.

### SEO review

Before publication, confirm search intent matches the page, title and description are unique,
heading order is logical, image metadata is useful, internal links are natural, structured facts
are supported, and the page offers something more original than existing summaries.

## 10. Publishing Checklist

The editor checks every applicable item. “Not applicable” requires a reason for safety-, rights-,
or accessibility-related items.

### Assignment and ownership

- [ ] The content type and editorial purpose are clear.
- [ ] Writer, editor, fact reviewer, photographer, and publisher are named.
- [ ] A qualified specialist reviewer is assigned where required.
- [ ] Conflicts, complimentary access, sponsorship, or commercial relationships are recorded.
- [ ] Consent and contributor expectations are agreed before sensitive reporting begins.

### Reporting and sources

- [ ] The writer or named contributor has direct knowledge appropriate to the piece.
- [ ] Every consequential factual claim has a source and verification date.
- [ ] GPS, fees, hours, road access, and dive conditions meet Section 8 where applicable.
- [ ] Names, spelling, titles, local terms, quotations, and translations are checked.
- [ ] Uncertainty, seasonal variation, and knowledge limits are stated honestly.
- [ ] Sensitive coordinates, community details, habitats, and private access are protected.
- [ ] Research notes and source records are stored in the approved editorial location.

### Story and voice

- [ ] The article has one clear editorial proposition.
- [ ] The introduction establishes a specific place, scene, or reason to read.
- [ ] The body contains original observation or attributed local knowledge.
- [ ] Practical information is distinct from narrative and easy to scan.
- [ ] The closing deepens the story without adding a generic CTA.
- [ ] Copy follows the brand voice and avoids clickbait, clichés, AI patterns, stuffing, and
      unsupported superlatives.
- [ ] No paragraph exists only to reach a length or keyword target.
- [ ] Joshua's Point recommendations are useful, conditional, and free of commercial pressure.

### Specialist standards

- [ ] Destination structure is complete where applicable.
- [ ] Dive technical and safety information has qualified local review.
- [ ] Scooter route has been checked with route, vehicle, date, weather, and return context recorded.
- [ ] Marine-life language describes observations without promising sightings.
- [ ] Route or dive difficulty is contextual and not framed as an achievement.
- [ ] Nearby recommendations form a realistic journey, not merely a geometric cluster.

### Photography and rights

- [ ] Images are original or properly licensed for the intended web and paid-product uses.
- [ ] Photographer, copyright, credit, consent, restrictions, and expiry are recorded.
- [ ] Hero and gallery images represent the actual place and current editorial story.
- [ ] Editing remains natural and documentary changes are disclosed.
- [ ] Every meaningful image has context-specific alt text.
- [ ] Captions add factual context and do not repeat alt text.
- [ ] Information-rich images have an adequate text equivalent.
- [ ] Public files do not expose sensitive metadata or precise locations unintentionally.

### Accessibility and readability

- [ ] Heading levels form a logical outline.
- [ ] Link text makes sense out of context.
- [ ] Lists and tables are used only when they improve comprehension.
- [ ] Instructions do not rely only on color, position, image, sound, or map interaction.
- [ ] Units, abbreviations, local terms, and difficulty labels are explained.
- [ ] Paragraphs and sentences are readable without oversimplifying the subject.
- [ ] Video has captions/transcript and audio has a transcript when present.
- [ ] A meaningful non-map location and orientation fallback exists.

### Relationships and SEO

- [ ] Slug and canonical subject are correct and unique.
- [ ] Title, excerpt, SEO title, description, and social image are accurate and distinct.
- [ ] Internal links are relevant, descriptive, published, and current enough to recommend.
- [ ] Manual relationships are intentional and contain no self-reference or duplicates.
- [ ] Structured-data facts are visible and supported by the page.
- [ ] No keyword repetition or metadata claim exceeds the evidence.

### CMS and preview

- [ ] Required fields and validation pass.
- [ ] Workflow status is approved before publication.
- [ ] Referenced content is published or intentionally scheduled.
- [ ] Mobile and desktop previews preserve meaning, crop, reading order, and captions.
- [ ] Empty optional sections collapse without leaving empty headings.
- [ ] Published and last-reviewed dates are accurate.
- [ ] The next fact-review date and owner are recorded.

### Final live verification

- [ ] The published URL resolves and the canonical URL is correct.
- [ ] Text, images, captions, credits, maps/fallbacks, and related links render correctly.
- [ ] Keyboard and screen-reader spot checks find no blocking issue.
- [ ] Metadata and social preview are accurate.
- [ ] No draft-only or sensitive information is exposed.
- [ ] Corrections contact or process is reachable.
- [ ] The quality score passes Section 11 and reviewer approval is recorded.

## 11. Editorial Quality Score

The score creates a shared review language. It does not turn editorial judgment into a leaderboard
or compensate for a failed safety, rights, or verification gate.

### Scoring scale

Use whole numbers from 1 to 10 for each dimension.

| Score | Meaning |
| ---: | --- |
| 1–2 | Unusable: absent, misleading, copied, inaccessible, or unsafe |
| 3–4 | Weak: major gaps, generic work, poor evidence, or substantial rewrite required |
| 5–6 | Serviceable draft: understandable but incomplete, uneven, or insufficiently distinctive |
| 7 | Publishable foundation: sound and useful, with minor improvement still possible |
| 8 | Strong: accurate, specific, considered, and clearly Joshua's Point |
| 9 | Exceptional: memorable original work with excellent reporting and execution |
| 10 | Reference quality: rare, enduring work that sets the standard for future pieces |

A score of 10 should be unusual. Reviewers score the published outcome, not effort or intention.

### Review dimensions

#### Accuracy

Are claims verified, current enough, qualified, traceable, and safe? Are facts clearly separated
from observation and opinion?

#### Storytelling

Does the piece have a clear proposition, specific scenes or observations, meaningful structure,
and a closing that earns its place?

#### Photography

Do the images reveal the place honestly, support the narrative, meet technical and rights
standards, and avoid repetition or spectacle for its own sake?

#### SEO

Does the page own a clear subject, use natural language and links, provide truthful metadata, and
offer original value likely to remain useful?

#### Accessibility

Can people understand and navigate the piece across devices and assistive technologies? Are text,
images, links, maps, media, structure, and practical information accessible?

#### Originality

Could this piece only come from Joshua's Point's observation, relationships, photography, and
editorial point of view—or could it have been assembled from generic sources?

#### Practical usefulness

Does the reader gain the dated, contextual information needed to decide, prepare, arrive, behave
responsibly, and know what still requires verification?

### Calculation and publish threshold

Use the unweighted mean of the seven dimensions, rounded to one decimal. Keep individual scores
visible; the average must never hide a critical weakness.

Publication requires:

- Overall score of **8.0 or higher**.
- Accuracy score of **8 or higher**.
- Accessibility score of **8 or higher**.
- Practical usefulness score of **8 or higher** for destinations, dive sites, and scooter routes.
- No dimension below **7**.
- Every applicable publishing checklist item complete.
- No unresolved safety, rights, consent, factual, privacy, or conflict-of-interest blocker.

A strategically important piece that does not meet the threshold remains a draft. The standard is
not lowered because a date, campaign, or search opportunity is approaching.

### Review record

Store:

- Content title and stable identifier.
- Version or review date.
- Seven scores and short evidence notes.
- Required changes.
- Reviewer and specialist reviewer.
- Final approval decision.
- Next fact-review date.

Use score trends to improve briefing, training, research, and photography—not to rank writers.

## 12. Future AI Guidelines

AI may support the editorial process, but it is not a witness, local source, fact checker, rights
holder, cultural authority, or final editor. It must never replace first-hand observation and local
knowledge.

### Permitted assistance

With human supervision, AI may help:

- Generate research questions and field-interview prompts.
- Organize notes that a human has already collected.
- Identify gaps, contradictions, ambiguous wording, or claims needing sources.
- Suggest structural alternatives for an existing draft.
- Proofread grammar, spelling, consistency, and reading level.
- Check a draft against this handbook and produce a review checklist.
- Propose metadata variants derived strictly from approved copy.
- Assist with transcripts, captions, or translations that receive human and language review.
- Compare a current fact sheet with an earlier one to flag possible changes.

### Prohibited uses

AI must never:

- Invent or infer GPS coordinates, fees, hours, travel times, access, road quality, dive conditions,
  depth, visibility, current, marine life, safety advice, quotations, history, or local customs.
- Claim to have visited, photographed, ridden, dived, tasted, interviewed, or observed anything.
- Create a composite anecdote and present it as a real event.
- Replace qualified dive, legal, medical, safety, cultural, translation, or local review.
- Fabricate sources, citations, consent, credentials, or confidence.
- Rewrite another publication closely enough to reproduce its voice, structure, or protected work.
- Generate documentary photographs or undisclosed alterations presented as reality.
- Publish directly or change `lastReviewedAt` without a human material review.
- Use private guest, contributor, source, unpublished location, or personal data in an unapproved
  external model or service.

### AI-assisted workflow

1. A human defines the assignment and gathers original material.
2. Source notes and verified facts are clearly marked before AI receives any approved excerpt.
3. The prompt states that unknowns must remain unknown and requests explicit fact flags.
4. AI output is treated as an untrusted draft.
5. The writer rewrites from actual knowledge and checks every claim against the fact sheet.
6. The editor checks voice, specificity, originality, privacy, and unsupported inference.
7. Required local and specialist reviewers approve the same material as any human-only draft.
8. The editorial record notes material AI assistance when governance requires it.

### AI disclosure and provenance

Internal editorial records should identify material AI use, the task performed, the responsible
human, and the review completed. Public disclosure should follow applicable law, platform rules,
and editorial judgment; it never substitutes for internal accountability.

### Core rule

> AI may help shape language and find questions. Only accountable people may establish what is
> true, what is safe to publish, and what Joshua's Point believes is worth saying.

## Recommended editorial roles

One person may hold several roles during the early project, but the responsibilities should remain
distinct.

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Commissioning editor | Chooses subject, purpose, audience, scope, risk, and contributors |
| Writer/reporter | Gathers original material, maintains notes, writes, and resolves fact queries |
| Photographer | Creates and captions truthful imagery; supplies rights and technical records |
| Fact reviewer | Checks claims, sources, dates, uncertainty, and review schedule |
| Specialist reviewer | Approves domain-specific material such as dive conditions or route safety |
| Copy editor | Refines structure, voice, grammar, consistency, and clarity without changing facts |
| Accessibility reviewer | Checks structure, alternatives, readability, media, maps, and preview |
| Publisher | Confirms approval, publishes, checks the live page, and records next review |

The person who wrote a consequential claim should not be its only fact reviewer. At minimum, dive
and safety-related material requires an independent qualified review.

## Corrections policy

- Provide one visible route for readers and local sources to report corrections.
- Acknowledge material reports promptly and record an owner.
- Qualify or unpublish immediately when a credible report creates a safety, access, privacy, or
  ecological concern.
- Correct the canonical fact and identify every page, guide, caption, relationship, or product that
  repeats it.
- Record what changed, why, when, and who verified it.
- Do not silently rewrite a material error or alter an original publication date.
- Thank sources when appropriate without exposing private reporters.

## Before the first real destination

Complete these steps before commissioning publication copy:

1. Select one destination with safe, lawful access and enough first-hand knowledge for a complete
   pilot.
2. Assign writer, photographer, fact reviewer, and local contact; assign a specialist if needed.
3. Create the fact-sheet and source-log template outside the public narrative fields.
4. Approve controlled vocabularies for route difficulty, road quality, transport, fee status, and
   review state.
5. Define the destination's sensitive-location and coordinate-precision decision.
6. Conduct one field visit focused on journey, arrival, access, photography, and practical facts;
   revisit if weather or route direction materially changes the experience.
7. Obtain rights, consent, and commercial-disclosure records before drafting around people or
   businesses.
8. Draft the full story, then create excerpt, caption, metadata, and related-content language from
   the approved narrative.
9. Run the checklist and quality score with a reviewer who did not write the piece.
10. Record a 90-day volatile-fact review and an annual full editorial review before publishing.

The pilot should be used to test this handbook. Improve the process where real editorial work
reveals ambiguity, but do not lower its trust, safety, accessibility, or originality standards.
