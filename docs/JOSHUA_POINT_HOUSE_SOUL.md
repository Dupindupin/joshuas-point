# Joshua's Point — House Soul

**Document type:** Internal editorial reference  
**Status:** Foundation draft; owner memories and architectural facts still required  
**Public copy:** No

## Purpose

This document protects the meaning of Joshua's Point while its public story is developed. It is a
source for editors, photographers, designers, and future contributors—not finished website copy.

Everything recorded here must come from one of two places:

1. An owner memory or observation, preserved without embellishment.
2. An approved Joshua's Point principle already established in the project documentation.

An empty space is preferable to an invented detail. Private family memories remain private unless
the owner explicitly approves their use.

## Source status

The project currently establishes that Joshua's Point was created from memories, love, and the bond
between Tobias and his wife. It also establishes that nature is the hero, architecture frames the
landscape, and guests should feel welcomed rather than sold to.

The detailed origin story and lived memories of the house have not yet been recorded. Sections
labelled **Owner source needed** must not be completed through inference from the building, existing
placeholder website copy, or generic hospitality language.

## 1. The origin of Joshua's Point

### How the land created the dream

The story begins with the land, before it becomes a story about a house. The future account should
record what the owners encountered there and how that encounter made a life on the site imaginable.
It should not turn the moment into a founding myth or imply details that were not consciously felt
at the time.

**Owner source needed**

- How the land was first found.
- What was present on the first visit.
- The first physical detail that held the owners' attention.
- When interest in the land became the idea of building a home.
- Whether that feeling arrived immediately or grew over repeated visits.

### The first emotional connection

The approved foundation is personal: Joshua's Point grew from memories, love, and the bond between
Tobias and his wife. The private story does not need to become public content. Internally, it may
guide the care, warmth, and sense of meaning that the editorial work must protect.

**Owner source needed**

- The first memory of standing on the land together.
- What felt different from other places.
- Which parts of this memory may remain internal and which, if any, may inform public writing.

### The relationship between sea and mountains

Sea and mountains belong to the identity of Joshua's Point. Their relationship should be described
from the owners' actual view and lived orientation—not from a map or an assumption about what the
property overlooks.

**Owner source and factual verification needed**

- The exact sea, islands, ridges, mountains, towns, or other landmarks visible from the property.
- Where each is seen from within the house and grounds.
- How weather, time of day, and season change their visibility.
- Which geographical names are accurate and appropriate to publish.

## 2. The philosophy of the house

### Nature is the hero

Joshua's Point does not use nature as a decorative background. Light, weather, sea, mountains,
vegetation, air, and changing conditions lead the experience. Architecture, photography, and words
should help people notice them.

### The house frames the landscape

The house should never compete with its setting. Its editorial role is to reveal the landscape
through openings, thresholds, shade, views, and the daily movement between rooms and the outdoors.
The building may be described carefully, but its size or status is not the story.

Architectural claims about orientation, slope, ventilation, materials, or environmental performance
must be verified with the owner and, where appropriate, the architect before publication.

### Indoor and outdoor living

Indoor and outdoor life are connected rather than treated as separate experiences. The deck, open
sliding doors, shared living spaces, roof, and pool all contribute to that relationship. Their
meaning should ultimately be explained through real use: where people sit, cook, talk, watch rain,
or move through the day.

**Owner source needed**

- Which doors are normally open and under what conditions.
- How the living room, kitchen, dining area, deck, and pool are used together.
- What changes during heat, wind, rain, and evening.

## 3. The feeling we protect

The owner's three foundation words are:

### Homy

**Owner wording: “Homy.”**

Joshua's Point should feel lived in and welcoming. Editorial work should protect the sense of a home
shared with care, not convert the house into a display object or an anonymous accommodation product.
The owner's own examples of what makes it homy still need to be recorded.

### Connected to nature

Connection should be expressed through specific, observed experiences: a view from a particular
place, doors opening, wind moving through a room, rain reaching the edge of the deck, or changing
light. Generic statements about nature are not enough.

### Peaceful

Peace does not mean that the place is silent or untouched by weather. It may include wind, rain,
birds, water, conversation, or long periods without a schedule. The real sounds, rhythms, and quiet
places must come from owner memory.

These feelings are editorial tests. A sentence or image that feels boastful, staged, crowded, or
pressuring works against them.

## 4. The guest experience

This section is a framework for gathering lived source material. It does not yet describe a typical
stay, because the supporting owner memories have not been recorded.

### Arrival feeling

Record the real sequence of arrival: the approach, the first view of the house or landscape, the
first threshold crossed, and the moment the body begins to slow down.

**Owner source needed:** What guests usually notice first, what the owner hopes they feel, and what
actually happens during a welcome.

### Morning rhythm

Morning should be documented through a real routine rather than an idealized itinerary.

**Owner source needed:** Where light arrives first, where coffee or breakfast happens, which doors
open, what can be heard, and whether people gather or spend time alone.

### Shared moments

The house is shaped for time with friends and family. The editorial story should focus on ordinary,
recognizable moments rather than staged sociability.

**Owner source needed:** Where people naturally gather, what is cooked, how the kitchen and dining
area are used, where conversation moves after a meal, and one shareable memory that feels true to
the house.

### Rainy days

Rain is part of the atmosphere, not an interruption that the story should hide. It should be
represented honestly, including both beauty and practical effects.

**Owner source and factual verification needed:** How rain sounds and looks from the house, which
spaces remain open or sheltered, how the deck and pool change, what people do, and whether access or
house systems are affected.

### Evening atmosphere

Evening should close the daily rhythm without becoming a generic sunset description.

**Owner source needed:** Where people sit, whether the sea or mountains remain visible, how the
interior is lit, what sounds replace the daytime sounds, and what a normal evening with friends or
family feels like.

## 5. Architecture principles

These principles describe the approved editorial territory. Exact design intent, dimensions,
materials, environmental performance, and authorship remain subject to verification.

### Deck

The deck is a central threshold between the house and the landscape. Project guidance already uses
the image of a visitor imagining themselves standing there as a measure of success. Its public story
must come from how the owners genuinely use it: mornings, weather, gatherings, quiet, and movement
between inside and outside.

### Open sliding doors

Open sliding doors allow interior rooms to participate in the conditions outside. The editorial
story should document when they remain open, how they change the rooms, and what practical limits
weather introduces.

### Connected living spaces

The living room, kitchen, and dining area belong to one shared rhythm. They should be understood
through cooking, eating, conversation, rest, and the way groups move through the house—not through
room counts or claims about scale.

### Roof

The roof contributes to shelter, shade, and the relationship between openness and weather. Its
exact form, design purpose, material, and performance have not yet been documented and must not be
inferred from placeholder copy.

### Pool relationship

The pool belongs within the wider relationship between architecture, deck, water, and view. Its
placement and actual role in daily life still need to be described by the owner. Avoid treating it
as an isolated amenity.

### Indoor/outdoor concept

The indoor/outdoor concept is not a slogan. It should be demonstrated through real thresholds,
weather, air, shade, shared activity, and daily routines. Photography should show these
relationships at human height and in natural use rather than simply documenting the building.

**Architecture verification needed**

- Architect, collaborators, and original brief.
- Site conditions that shaped the design.
- Orientation and verified view directions.
- Roof, shade, ventilation, drainage, and weather response.
- Actual timber, stone, glazing, and principal construction materials.
- Deck and pool placement, use, and maintenance.

## 6. What Joshua's Point is not

### Not a party place

Joshua's Point protects peace, the house, nature, neighbours, and the quality of time shared there.
Editorial imagery should not present loud groups, event-scale gatherings, or the property as a
backdrop for spectacle. Any eventual house rule must be stated separately and approved by the
owner; this principle does not invent an operating policy.

### Not focused on luxury status

The story is not about labels, exclusivity, awards, or comparison with other properties. Comfort
and care may be described through real details, but words such as “luxury,” “exclusive,” “premium,”
and “world-class” do not express the soul of the house.

### Not about showing size

Photography should not distort space or use scale as proof of value. Writing should not lead with
floor area, grandeur, capacity, or abundance. The meaningful measure is how architecture helps
people live with the landscape and with one another.

## 7. Editorial rules

1. **Protect private family stories.** Internal knowledge is not automatic permission to publish.
   Ask what may be shared, in what form, and whether the people involved consent.
2. **Never invent memories.** Do not add weather, dialogue, feelings, gestures, routines, or origin
   details because they would make a better story.
3. **Nature remains the hero.** Architecture, materials, people, and hospitality should direct
   attention toward the living conditions of the place.
4. **Write from real experiences only.** Prefer one precise observation to a broad atmospheric
   claim. If nobody has observed it, leave it out.
5. **Separate memory from fact.** A remembered feeling may be preserved as memory. Geography,
   materials, design authorship, building systems, access, capacity, and operating policies require
   factual verification.
6. **Preserve the owner's voice.** Keep distinctive wording, including simple or imperfect phrases,
   until the owner approves any edit. Clarify without making the voice generic.
7. **Do not exaggerate.** Do not intensify a true memory with superlatives, invented permanence, or
   promises about how every guest will feel.
8. **Show life, not performance.** Favor real cooking, conversation, reading, swimming, rain,
   silence, and changing light over staged aspiration.
9. **Do not sell inside the story.** This document exists to protect meaning, not produce claims,
   calls to action, or booking language.
10. **Keep unknowns visible.** Mark missing owner source, factual verification, sensitivity review,
    and publication approval rather than smoothing over the gap.
